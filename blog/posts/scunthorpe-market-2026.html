Scunthorpe Property Market 2026: Prices, Rents and Yields — The Numbers

2026-10-01

Scunthorpe rarely makes national property headlines, and that's exactly why investors like it: while everyone argues about London and Manchester, North Lincolnshire keeps quietly doing what it has always done — offering entry prices most of the country can't believe, with rents that hold up. Let's look at the actual numbers as they stand in 2026.

What properties are selling for

Land Registry-based figures put the average Scunthorpe sold price at roughly £155,000–£156,000 in early 2026 — and notably, prices have been flat to slightly down over the last year (aggregators show somewhere between -2% and -5% depending on the sample). Meanwhile the wider North Lincolnshire average sits around £180,000 (ONS, mid-2026) and actually rose about 4.5% year-on-year.

Two things follow from that:

1. Scunthorpe itself is a buyer's negotiation window right now. Flat prices plus motivated sellers is the combination bargain-hunters wait for — particularly at auction.

2. The averages hide huge variance by size. Roughly: two-bed terraces and semis around £140,000, three-beds around £175,000–£180,000, four-bed detached stock £300,000+. The investor market lives in the first two bands.

Properties take around four to six months to sell on the open market — slow by national standards, which is precisely why auction and below-market routes exist here, and why they work.

What tenants are paying

The ONS private rental figures for North Lincolnshire (August 2026) show an average of £653 per calendar month, broken down as:

| Bedrooms | Average rent (pcm) |

|---|---|

| 1 bed | £472 |

| 2 bed | £616 |

| 3 bed | £751 |

| 4+ bed | £1,058 |

Scunthorpe-specific trackers put average house rents higher — around £830 pcm, with two-bed houses near £715 and three-beds near £836 — because the town's stock is dominated by houses (54% semi-detached, 27% terraced), not flats.

Rents have been rising faster than prices here for a couple of years, which is the quiet engine behind the yield story below.

The yield arithmetic

Gross yield = annual rent ÷ purchase price. Using the figures above:

  • Typical two-bed at £141,000 letting at £616–£715 pcm → 5.2%–6.1% gross
  • Three-bed at £178,000 letting at £751 pcm → around 5.1% gross
  • Cheaper terraced stock bought at auction below £120,000, letting around £600+ pcm → 6%+ gross is genuinely achievable

Compare that with the ~4–4.5% gross typical of many southern cities, and you can see why cashflow investors keep circling this postcode. Remember gross yield ignores costs — management, voids, maintenance, licensing if you go HMO — so always run the net number before you fall in love.

What I'd take from all this

  • Flat prices aren't failure here — they're the entry opportunity. Capital growth has historically been modest; this is a cashflow market, and you should buy it as one.
  • Buy for the tenant, not for yourself. Two- and three-bed houses in DN15/DN16 with outdoor space are the workhorses of the local lettings market.
  • Watch the auctions. BTG Eddisons and Savills regularly catalogue DN15–DN20 lots — our [live auction map](../index.html) tracks the unsold ones automatically.

All figures are drawn from public sources (ONS Private Rental Index, Land Registry-based aggregators and listing trackers) as of late 2026, they move, and they average over very different streets. Verify every number against the specific property before you act. Nothing here is financial advice.
